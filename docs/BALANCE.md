# Understory — Balance

Every tuning constant in the game, with where it came from.

Values live in `assets/data/balance.ron`; this file grades them. A test
(`crates/core/src/tests/balance_doc.rs`) fails if a constant exists in one and not the
other, in either direction — so this table cannot quietly rot.

## Grades

| Grade | Means |
|---|---|
| `DESIGNED` | Chosen from reasoning about the intended feel. Plausible, unproven. |
| `PLAYTESTED` | Someone played with it, and with neighbouring values, and this one won. |

M5 does not ship until every row reads `PLAYTESTED`. Being wrong fast is the point of
having the table; being wrong *quietly* is what the grade prevents.

---

## World

The tower's gait and how far it can see.

> **`yield_pct` and `sun_pct` are one constant in two columns. Never tune either alone.**
>
> They are deliberately opposed — shade is biomass-rich and sun-poor, open ruin field the
> reverse — and `SYSTEMS.md` §5.11 open question 0 spent a milestone reading one half of that
> pair in isolation and concluding the ground was decorative. It is not. Terrain intake is
> paid in **ground covered**, not in ticks, and a browned-out tower stops walking: shade buys
> richer ground and less power to cross it. Measured across twelve seeds and both route
> policies, bamboo harvested in a fixed 109,520 ticks:
>
> | tower | shade | sun | gap |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | bare, with a kitchen | 6501 | 6412 | +1.4% |
> | + garden, comb, ropery | 5596 | 5702 | −1.9% |
> | + the same, with a chute | 3568 | 3632 | −1.8% |
> | + the same, with a burner | 3672 | 3601 | +2.0% |
> | **bare, bottomless buffers** | **7988** | **8099** | **−1.4%** |
>
> The last row is a ceiling: buffers so large nothing can ever jam, so the cutter arm never
> stalls. **Uncapping a tower is worth a quarter of its harvest and nothing on the route.**
> The two sides cancel to within two percent on every tower shape, which is a dead heat, and
> a dead heat is exactly what the opposition was written to produce. Raising a `yield_pct`
> without lowering the matching `sun_pct` does not make that band better — it makes the pair
> stop being a trade.
>
> **Three cautions for anyone tuning these.** Per seed the same comparison swings from −3.9%
> to +71.9%, so a single-seed reading is a coin flip; eight seeds is not enough either, having
> given +9.7% on two outliers. Let each route run for a fixed tick count, not to the region
> edge, or the two walk different distances — a confound sitting on the axis being measured.
> And change something that should not matter (build order, storeroom count) to check a number
> survives it: `journey.rs` has produced more findings about itself than about the game.
>
> A sink for the surplus — continuous overgrowth, giving poles demand that scales with the
> tower the way meals scale with the crew — was **prototyped, measured and left out**: it
> means a tower is never quite whole, so "something is attacking" and "it is Tuesday" stop
> being distinguishable on the standing readout. Commit `4a4683a` has the implementation. It
> would raise harvest; the table above says it would not change the route.

| Constant | Value | Grade | Reasoning |
|---|---:|---|---|
| `stride_paces_per_100_ticks` | 60 | DESIGNED | 0.6 paces/tick, 18 a second. Fast enough that the horizon changes while you watch a chain run; slow enough that terrain feels like somewhere rather than something flickering past. Becomes a player throttle in M3. |
| `band_min_paces` | 300 | DESIGNED | ~16 s in a band at 1×. Below this, terrain changes faster than a haul round-trip, so route choice could never bite. |
| `band_max_paces` | 900 | DESIGNED | ~50 s at 1×, ~12 s at 4×. Long enough that a rich band is worth noticing and a barren one is worth waiting out. |
| `stream_ahead_paces` | 900 | DESIGNED | One full maximum band beyond the tower, so the horizon is never visibly generated into existence. |
| `stream_behind_paces` | 300 | DESIGNED | Enough to keep what has just passed on screen for parallax; anything older is dropped. |

## Tower

Shape, height, and the price of both.

| Constant | Value | Grade | Reasoning |
|---|---:|---|---|
| `starting_floors` | 4 | DESIGNED | Enough vertical distance that the opening haul is a climb, few enough that the tower reads at a glance on the first frame. |
| `max_floors` | 14 | DESIGNED | The top of the cozy 8–14 range from `v2-plan.md` §0. Above this the cross-section stops fitting on one screen, which is the constraint the whole art direction rests on. |
| `floor_slots` | 8 | DESIGNED | Wide enough for a three-wide room beside a two-wide and a one-wide, with the stairs column already taking one — so a floor is a packing decision rather than a fixed set of four. Tight enough that a shaft is a real sacrifice, which is the entire point of shafts costing floor width. Was reasoned about as "four two-wide rooms" when every room *was* two wide; see `SYSTEMS.md` §0.5 for the widths as they now stand. |
| `floor_cost` | 6 poles | DESIGNED | About two minutes of the opening chain's output. Growing taller should be a decision you save up for, not a button you press. |
| `stairs_capacity` | 1 | DESIGNED | One body at a time. With two crew this is already a queue, which puts the contention thesis on screen in the first minute rather than in M1. |
| `starting_stock` | 10 poles | DESIGNED | One floor's worth plus a room, so the first build is possible before the mill has ever run, and the second is not. |

## Crew

How fast the tower's pulse beats.

| Constant | Value | Grade | Reasoning |
|---|---:|---|---|
| `crew_cap` | 8 | DESIGNED | The ceiling an enclave may recruit the tower up to. **Was 6, deferring the eight `v2-plan.md` §6.6 eventually wants to "whatever M4's shift rota earns". The rota earns it.** Eight crew split across two shifts is about five awake at once, which is roughly where six unrota'd crew already sat — so raising the cap without the rota would have been a straight throughput gift, and with it it buys *coverage* instead: the hours a tower is least able to answer a wave are exactly the hours the night prowler is out, and staffing them costs daylight throughput on the shift where the chain actually flows. The old row's reasoning, kept because it still sizes the number: M3 can only move one place toward the cap — the drowned city's enclave offers exactly one recruit (see Content constants below) — so this is headroom for M4 and M5 rather than a limit a run will press against; it exists now because `Recruit` has to reject *something*, and "the tower is full" needs a number to be full against. Sized against `starting_crew`'s measurement rather than guessed: three crew moved throughput ninety percent over two, so a cap of six is two more doublings of that kind of change, which is as much as the stairs-and-shafts contention this game is built on should have to absorb before a shift rota exists to organise it. |
| `starting_crew` | 3 | PLAYTESTED | Was 2. Measured over 600 s (`cargo run --release -p understory-core --example throughput`): with two crew, adding an elevator changed crafted output by less than noise, so M1's sprint question could not be answered at all. At three, the same comparison shows **+90% crafts and 56% fewer ticks spent queueing**. Two crew make a queue; three make it matter. Re-measured after M3's intake fix, which changed the absolute rates the original figures were taken against: **+56% crafts (34 to 53) and 59% fewer ticks queueing (1,174 to 479)** over the same 600 s window. Less dramatic than the +90% first recorded and pointing the same way, which is what matters — the elevator's value is that it unblocks a queue, and the queue got shorter by more than it did before. |
| `walk_ticks_per_slot` | 12 | DESIGNED | 0.4 s a slot, ~3 s to cross a floor. Horizontal distance is a real but minor cost, so layout matters without dominating. |
| `climb_ticks_per_floor` | 30 | DESIGNED | A full second a floor: two and a half times the cost of walking the same "distance". Vertical is expensive, which is why a dumbwaiter will be worth its slot in M1. |
| `load_ticks` | 15 | DESIGNED | Half a second at each end. Long enough to read as an action in the cross-section, short enough not to dominate a round trip. |
| `unload_ticks` | 15 | DESIGNED | Symmetric with loading; no reason yet for them to differ. |
| `carry_capacity` | 3 | DESIGNED | Sets haul throughput against production: two crew at three per trip roughly match the opening chain, so the tower runs but has no slack. That margin is the tension. |
| `stress_ticks` | 60 | DESIGNED | Two seconds blocked before a crew member tints red. Short enough to catch a real bottleneck, long enough that ordinary traffic doesn't cry wolf. |
| `hungry_ticks` | 4800 | DESIGNED | A third of a day, so three meals a day a person. Short enough that the canteen is somewhere people actually go, long enough that eating is not most of what a crew member does. At the canteen's 2 bamboo a meal that is **6 bamboo a day a crew member** — 24 for four crew, about a fifth of what a fed mill draws (one bamboo per 120 ticks is 120 a day) and a fifth of what one cutter arm brings in on region 1's palette. That ratio is the whole judgement: enough that a route rich in biomass visibly feeds people better, not so much that meals displace poles as what bamboo is *for*. |
| `starving_ticks` | 7200 | DESIGNED | Half a day — a full meal cycle *past* being hungry, and the gap between the two thresholds is the design rather than slack in it. If going-to-eat and slowing-down were one number, crew would trudge to the canteen constantly and a working kitchen would read as a permanent tax; with a third of a day between them the penalty appears only when the kitchen has actually failed — no bamboo, no canteen, or a canteen nobody can reach. **A fed tower never sees it.** Hunger is a supply problem, and it is the chain's failure you feel rather than the need itself. |
| `rested_max_ticks` | 8640 | DESIGNED | 0.6 of a day, a shade over the 8,352-tick day shift, so a day worker ends their shift nearly empty and a night worker (6,048 ticks awake) never does. The near-miss is deliberate: against 8,352 awake it leaves almost no slack, so **a bunked day-shift crew member crosses `tired_ticks` about four-fifths of the way through every shift and works the last stretch slowly.** The tower visibly tires in the late afternoon and picks up at dawn. That is not an oversight to tune out — it is the day having a shape, which is most of what "somewhere people live" means on screen, and it costs nothing but the relationship between two constants. |
| `tired_ticks` | 1440 | DESIGNED | A tenth of a day of work left in them before they slow down. Paired with `rested_max_ticks` above to put the flag in the late afternoon; moving either moves where in the day the tower sags. |
| `rest_gain_per_tick` | 2 | DESIGNED | Two ticks of rest a tick asleep, so a night off shift (6,048 ticks) refills 12,096 against a cap of 8,640 — comfortably over, which is what makes a bunk feel like a solved problem rather than a managed one. A bed should be something you build once and stop thinking about; the interesting decision is the rota, not the bed. |
| `no_bunk_rest_gain` | 1 | DESIGNED | Sleeping on the deck where you stopped. The arithmetic is unforgiving and exact: a day-shift sleeper on the floor banks 6,048 against the 8,352 they spend, a net loss of 2,304 a day, so they slide into permanent tiredness inside four days and never climb out. Survivable, visibly degrading, and fixable at any moment for three poles — the right shape for a cost the player is always allowed to stop paying. |
| `hungry_work_pct` | 60 | DESIGNED | Past `starving_ticks`, everything takes about two-thirds longer. Plainly slower on screen without reading as broken. Scales the *duration* of an action, never the fixed-point step that advances it — see `SYSTEMS.md` §4.4 and `intake::terrain_effort` for what the other arithmetic costs. |
| `tired_work_pct` | 60 | DESIGNED | Deliberately the same number as hunger's: one visible failure mode with two causes, so a player learns the *look* of a crew member working badly once and then asks why, rather than learning two separate symptoms. |
| `dark_work_pct` | 75 | DESIGNED | The mildest of the three, and the one that connects the rota to charge. A crew member working while `Power.lit` is false — a brown-out, not merely a dark night — works by feel, just not well. This is what makes a night shift's dependence on charge sharp without adding a single new charge sink: a brown-out does not just dim the tower, it wastes the shift you staffed. The alternative — gating lighting on somebody being awake — was considered and rejected in `SYSTEMS.md` §4.4, because the default rota is all-Day and it would have made most towers' lamps free and quietly relaxed the brown-out pressure the Power rows below were measured against. Composed with the other two the worst case is 27%: crawling, never stopped, because a need that halts the tower is a death spiral rather than a pressure. |

## Clock

The day, and what reads it.

| Constant | Value | Grade | Reasoning |
|---|---:|---|---|
| `ticks_per_day` | 14400 | DESIGNED | 8 minutes at 1x (480 s x 30 ticks/s), ~2 minutes at 4x — about 20 day cycles across a 3-hour run, enough for the sun/route tension to repeat many times without a single day feeling like the whole session. |
| `sun_curve` | 8 anchors, 0-0-40-90-100-60-10-0 | DESIGNED | A smooth rise and fall rather than a staircase, so no daypart boundary reads as a lighting bug. Dark for the first tenth of the day, peaking at midday, dark again by the last fifteenth — integrating the curve over a full day (see Power below) gives an average exposure of ~46.5% on neutral terrain, the number the sail and cost figures below are sized against. |
| `night_light_threshold` | 20 | DESIGNED | Crossing this against the curve puts the "lights must be lit" span at ~330 permille of the day (~4,750 ticks, ~2.6 min) — long enough that unlit floors are a real cost, short enough that a competent tower shrugs off most nights. |

## Power

Charge income, storage, and draw. See `SYSTEMS.md` §1.2.

| Constant | Value | Grade | Reasoning |
|---|---:|---|---|
| `starting_charge` | 800 | DESIGNED | Tick 0 is predawn, so a fresh run starts in the dark. A starting tower's base draw (striding + lighting 4 floors, no thornwright yet) is ~0.28 charge/tick; 800 outlasts the ~2,200 ticks until the curve clears the light threshold, with room to spare, but is nowhere near enough to also run a thornwright unattended — the tower starts tight, not comfortable. |
| `stride_charge_per_100_ticks` | 20 | DESIGNED | Continuous striding costs 2,880 charge across a full day-cycle — real money next to a two-sail "good sun" day's ~20,000 charge income (see Content constants below), but not so much that walking is unaffordable. Visible and ongoing, which is what makes `SetStriding{false}` a genuine choice rather than a button nobody presses. |
| `light_charge_per_100_ticks_per_floor` | 2 | DESIGNED | Small per floor, but multiplies with height: an 8-floor tower lit through the ~4,750-tick night spends ~760 charge; a 4-floor starting tower spends ~380. Enough that a brown-out reads as "the tower outgrew what it can light," not as noise. |

## Transport

Dwell, dispatch, and the estimates crew use to pick a shaft. See `SYSTEMS.md` §1.3.

| Constant | Value | Grade | Reasoning |
|---|---:|---|---|
| `dwell_base_ticks` | 10 | DESIGNED | A third of a second of doors-and-landing overhead before anyone moves — enough to read as a stop in the cross-section, not enough to make every trip feel like a wait. |
| `dwell_per_unit_ticks` | 6 | DESIGNED | Cheaper than a full `load_ticks`/`unload_ticks` (15) — boarding a car is simpler than working a shelf — but it still adds up: a full 4-unit car adds 24 ticks to a stop, visibly slower than an empty one. |
| `dispatch_threshold` | 2 | DESIGNED | Matches `starting_crew`. With two crew, a car that leaves the instant the first caller arrives would never show contention at all, which is the exact thing M1's sprint question ("is elevator contention actually fun?") is testing. |
| `dispatch_max_wait_ticks` | 90 | DESIGNED | 3 seconds. A lone caller on a quiet floor still gets served eventually — batching is meant to create the queue a player can see, not to strand anyone waiting for a second caller who never comes. |
| `queue_penalty_ticks` | 60 | DESIGNED | A floor of 2 seconds per person ahead of you in a stairs estimate. Note the estimate multiplies this by the *queue length* rather than applying it once: a flat penalty had crew cheerfully joining a five-deep line because "occupied" cost the same whether one person or five were ahead. |
| `elevator_base_wait_ticks` | 45 | DESIGNED | 1.5 seconds, a rough midpoint between "the car is right here" and "the car is at the far end of its run." The estimate crew fall back on before the car's actual position and direction sharpen it. |

## Siege

> **Re-measured, and the grades stand.** These rows were tuned with `examples/siege_run.rs`'s
> three-tower narrative, which stopped being able to measure anything when M3 changed the
> economy: a tower only provokes while it is consuming, so once its shopping list runs out the
> jungle forgets it and every plan finishes untouched.
>
> The harness grew a **pressure table** for that — provocation pinned rather than earned, the
> tower's shape held fixed, poles and darts kept on the shelves, three days a run and six
> seeds a cell. Six, because one seed does not describe a curve: `min_provocation` lets the
> leaper in at 330 and the borer at 500, so the wave mix changes discontinuously and a single
> run reads as nonsense. Measured once, the starting tower was mauled at 600 and untouched at
> 1,000.
>
> What it says, averaged over six seeds after three days, with the fraction being seeds in
> which the Heartseed went:
>
> | tower | prov 100 | 300 | 500 | 700 | 1000 |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | as it starts | held | held | lost 1/6 | lost 3/6 | lost 5/6 |
> | plated twice | held | held | held | lost 3/6 | lost 4/6 |
> | battery + darts | held | held | held | lost 1/6 | lost 3/6 |
>
> That is the shape these rows were aiming for. Quiet play is safe outright. Both upgrades buy
> a whole band — the survivable ceiling moves from 300 to 500 — and above that the battery is
> what keeps a tower standing, cutting deaths from three in six to one in six at 700 and from
> five to three at 1,000. The top of the dial is close to fatal, which is right: you reach
> `provocation_max` by playing as loudly as the game allows, and it should be the last thing
> you do.
>
> **Two earlier readings of this table were wrong, both for one reason.** It briefly said the
> curve was far too steep — everything dead at 600 — and that plating was actively harmful.
> Both were downstream of a single bug: repair was gated on empty hands, so a crew member
> holding a load with nowhere to put it could never mend, and the harness had stuffed the
> shelves to keep the chain fed, manufacturing exactly that dead end. With it fixed the curve
> is the table above and plating is worse on none of eight seeds. The account is kept in the
> `reinforce` row below, because the failure mode is more instructive than the numbers: an
> instrument that changes the thing it measures produces a confident number, and a confident
> number is what gets a working feature deleted.
>
> **M4 moved the whole table down a band, and the reachable range moved with it.** Sleep takes
> about two-fifths of the tower's crew-hours out of the economy, and mending is crew-hours;
> re-measured on the same six seeds, a bare tower now reads *worn* at provocation 300 where it
> held, and loses the Heartseed in 3 of 6 seeds at 500 where it lost 1. Nothing in these rows
> caused that and none of them is the fix.
>
> What keeps it coherent is that **the provocation a real tower actually reaches fell by the
> same order.** Under the same measurement, the three narrative towers now peak at 6
> (subsistence), 56 (greedy) and 60 (answered) — against 27, 216 and 203 before M4. Play now
> lives in the 0–100 band, where every tower shape still holds outright, and the upper half of
> this table describes provocation no run currently earns. That is a defensible place to be
> and a fragile one: the moment M5's tier-two chains give a tower more to consume, the
> reachable band climbs back into the part of the curve that kills, and this table has to be
> read again rather than inherited.
>
> **The plating comparison came back "worse on 7 of 8 seeds" and is *not* being acted on.**
> That reading has been wrong twice before (see the `reinforce` row below), and the reason is
> in the metric rather than in the seeds: the harness compares *hit points still missing*
> between two towers whose maxima differ by exactly the plating being tested, so a plated tower
> starts with more to lose and reads worse for having it. The margin here is 308 against 322,
> under 5%. Recorded so the next person does not rediscover it as news; not a finding.
>
> These grades rest on a scripted pressure test, not on a person playing. Nothing here has
> been *felt* yet, and that is the honest limit on every `PLAYTESTED` in this section.


What creatures attack, how far ahead they appear, how often waves come, the one knob
(provocation) that scales all of it, and what putting the tower back together costs. See
`SYSTEMS.md` §2.

| Constant | Value | Grade | Reasoning |
|---|---:|---|---|
| `enemy_fade_ticks` | 20 | DESIGNED | Two thirds of a second at 1x, still a sixth of one at 4x. Before this, a creature that died or lost its grip existed for exactly one further tick — one frame at 1x, likely zero frames at 4x — so a kill was a number changing, not something the player could watch happen. `Dying` (shot down) and `Leaving` (lost its grip — see the `cling_ticks` rows in Content constants, below) both hold for this long before they are removed, and fade identically: the point is giving the player a frame to see the outcome, not grading it. Grading is `repelled`'s job, and it only counts `Dying`. |
| `panel_hp` | 150 | DESIGNED | A floor's outer skin — the first thing a wave reaches and the cheapest thing to lose. Sized just above what the smallest wave can do: `base_threat` 10 buys two skitters (see Content constants below), and two skitters clinging unanswered for their full grip do 96 between them, so the opening wave scrapes a panel rather than breaching it. Four skitters — 192 damage, what a more provoked run graduates to — take one down in well under a minute of unimpeded biting, which is the "an unprepared tower loses a panel" case this milestone is sized against. This row used to call four skitters "the entire opening-wave budget"; that was true of the unconditional `base_threat` of 20 the siege spec opened with and stale from the moment it was halved, and it is corrected here rather than quietly dropped. Repairing a panel from nothing costs 15 poles and 600 ticks of crew time — two and a half times a floor's own build cost (6 poles, see the Tower section above), a real but not crippling bill. |
| `room_hp` | 260 | DESIGNED | Not quite twice a panel's health. Rooms sit behind the skin, so reaching one should take real work once its floor's panel is already down, rather than the panel and the room behind it falling in the same few seconds. |
| `shaft_hp` | 600 | DESIGNED | Four times a panel's health. Severing a shaft is the signature emergency (`SYSTEMS.md` §2.3) and must never be one bite: a lone root-borer chewing uninterrupted (18 damage every 70 ticks) needs roughly 2,333 ticks — about 78 seconds — to take a shaft from full health to severed. That is real, sustained effort at one column, not a routine annoyance — and more than a lone borer actually gets to finish: its own `cling_ticks` (1,200, see Content constants below) runs out at roughly the halfway point, so on a tower that keeps walking, severing a shaft takes two overlapping borers, not one. |
| `heartseed_hp` | 4000 | DESIGNED | Well over six times a shaft column's health, and by far the toughest single thing in the tower. The Heartseed is the loss condition and is only ever targeted once nothing ordinary is left standing (`pick_target`'s fallback order) — nothing should reach it by accident, and nothing should finish it quickly once something does. |
| `spawn_paces_ahead` | 540 | DESIGNED | The tower strides 0.6 paces/tick (`stride_paces_per_100_ticks` above). 540 / 0.6 = 900 ticks = 30 s at 1x, comfortably inside a 20-40 s reaction window between the wave-arrival sound cue and contact. A creature's own approach speed adds to the tower's stride when closing the gap, so faster creatures (the night prowler) arrive sooner than this baseline and slower ones (the root-borer) later — the constant is sized against the tower's half of that closing speed, which is the one number every creature shares. |
| `wave_interval_ticks` | 4800 | PLAYTESTED | 160 seconds between wave checks — three a day-cycle (`ticks_per_day` 14400 / 4800), down from six. A four-skitter wave does about 192 damage, which costs about 19 poles to mend (`repair_poles_per_10_hp`, below); one mill makes about 20 poles every 2,400 ticks at its nominal craft rate, and fewer once bamboo supply and haul latency are counted (see `provocation_per_100_harvested`, below, on how far short of nominal a chain actually runs). At the old 2,400-tick interval, a tower's entire pole income went straight back into its own skin every single wave, it could never bank enough to build anything else, and a damaged mill turned that into a spiral it could not climb out of — a recorded run against the old value sat at 0 poles in the bank against a 120-pole repair bill. The doubled interval is confirmed by the three-tower, five-day comparison in `cargo run --release -p understory-core --example siege_run`, re-measured after M3's intake fix (see `provocation_per_100_harvested`, below, for the full shape of that run): the undefended `greedy` tower is pole-broke from day 3 and its outstanding repair bill climbs 4 → 80 → 101, but it is degraded rather than annihilated — it keeps mending 878 and then 820 hit points on days 4 and 5 out of the trickle one mill makes, and finishes at 860‰ standing. The `answered` tower never drops below 992‰ across all five days. Three waves a day is enough to keep a tower that will not defend itself visibly losing ground, and few enough that one which does defend itself is not simply out-spent. A five-day scripted run through one shopping list is one designed scenario, not a person probing the edges, so treat the grade as confirmed for the shape of play the harness exercises rather than exhaustively tuned. |
| `threat_per_100_provocation` | 40 | DESIGNED | Converts provocation into a wave's threat budget: `scaled = provocation * 40 / 100`. A wave check now spends `scaled` first against the cheapest eligible creature's threat — if it can't afford even that (skitter, threat 5, so below provocation 13) nothing spawns at all, which is the gate `base_threat` (below) used not to have. Once a wave clears that gate, the actual budget is `scaled.max(base_threat)`, not the two summed: at `provocation_max` (1000), `scaled` is 400 — forty times the `base_threat` floor of 10, and the richest wave the game throws. That escalation is what makes an aggressively-played, undefended run feel like it is heading somewhere bad, without the very first point of provocation buying a full wave the way an unconditional floor used to. |
| `base_threat` | 10 | PLAYTESTED | The floor under a wave's threat budget once one has already cleared the affordability gate in `threat_per_100_provocation` (above) — not, itself, a floor that guarantees a wave exists at all. With `threat_per_100_provocation` at 40 and the skitter (threat 5) the cheapest eligible creature, the earliest a wave can happen at all is provocation 13; from there, `base_threat` now guarantees the budget is never below 10, buying a pair of skitters rather than the four an unconditional floor of 20 used to guarantee from the very first point of provocation, regardless of how little the tower had done. That earlier version put a barely-harvesting tower under full siege before it had done anything worth answering for; halving the floor, on top of gating it behind an affordability check, means four skitters and beyond is now something a run earns by playing loudly, not the baseline every run opens with. The re-measured three-tower `siege_run` comparison (see `provocation_per_100_harvested`, below) is consistent with the smaller floor: none of the three towers loses a large share of its integrity to a single early wave. `subsistence` never rises above provocation 27 — barely over the wave gate of 13 — takes only the light scrapes the floor allows, and mends all 660 hit points of them for 74 poles inside three days, ending whole. `greedy`, which draws heavy provocation with no defence at all, still slides gradually rather than falling over: 1000‰, 1000‰, 993‰, 890‰, 860‰ across the five days. That is a shape the run's day-by-day standing figures support, not a direct measurement of wave composition, so the grade should be read with that limit in mind. |
| `provocation_max` | 1000 | DESIGNED | The ceiling every other provocation field is a fraction of. Round, so the canopy leaper's "about a third" (330) and the root-borer's "half" (500) gates land on clean numbers. |
| `provocation_per_100_harvested` | 300 | PLAYTESTED | 2.4 points an item, calibrated so that one cutter arm in region 1 sits on the line and the second one is what provokes. Against `provocation_decay_per_100_ticks`'s 288 points a day, break-even is exactly 120 items a day, and that is where one arm lands: measured over five days from seed `0x5EED0002` with shelf space deliberately not the limit, a one-arm tower cut 128, 118, 116, 108 and 98 stalks a day. So a subsistence tower straddles zero and drifts quiet, while a second arm pushes it decisively over. **The correction this row most needed:** it used to read 300 and to explain a measured ~101 items a day by saying "throughput is bounded by shelf space and crew legs, not by the arm". ## Journey

The handful of constants that belong to the run rather than to any one room or creature:
where a fork is allowed to fall, how near an enclave counts as berthed, and what a ruin
charges for what it holds. See `SYSTEMS.md` §3.3–§3.5.

| Constant | Value | Grade | Reasoning |
|---|---:|---|---|
| `fork_edge_margin_paces` | 5000 | DESIGNED | A third of a fork interval (15,000, see Content constants below). A fork inside this margin of either end of its region, or of the region's enclave, is skipped — a decision that lands on top of a boundary or a berth competes with it for the same stretch of horizon, and the player ends up answering two things at once when the whole argument for a *fixed* fork interval was that the rhythm should be learnable. Sized by the two cases it actually has to arbitrate. Region 2's enclave stands 8,000 paces in and its first fork at 15,000: 7,000 apart, so the margin has to be under that or the region's only fork disappears. And region 2's second fork at 30,000 against a length roll of 34,000–46,000 is 4,000 from the far edge at the short end and 16,000 at the long end, so a margin of 5,000 is exactly what makes the roll decide whether that fork exists — which is the mechanism §3.2 leans on to buy variety without making fork spacing itself unpredictable. Both jobs at once, from one number, is why it is 5,000 and not 3,000 or 8,000. |
| `enclave_berth_paces` | 80 | DESIGNED | About a five-second window at 1× and under two at 4× — the tower covers 80 paces in 133 ticks at `stride_paces_per_100_ticks` 60. A shade wider than the salvage rig's own 60-pace reach (Content constants below), because a rig is a room the player deliberately placed and an enclave is a thing the world put in front of them: the berth you have to *notice* deserves a slightly larger target than the one you built for. Not wide enough to stop by accident, which matters, since the enclave is behind you for the rest of the run once passed. If it reads as fiddly in play, the fix is a larger number here — not an approach-and-dock mechanic, which would make berthing a mode and cost §3.5 its "the transaction is a board, the berth is diegetic" split. |
| `warden_threat_per_100_salvage` | 120 | DESIGNED | The one number that prices a ruin, and it prices both sides at once. At a warden's threat of 30 (Content constants below), 120 per 100 units means one warden per 25 units of salvage: the 25-unit floor of a ruin's authored range rouses exactly one, a 50-unit ruin rouses two, and the top of the range at full richness rouses a handful. The payout and the price are therefore the *same* number, which is what makes "is this ruin worth stopping for" a question about the tower — its darts, its poles, its remaining panel health — rather than a question about the ruin, which would have a fixed answer. Floored at a single warden so a nearly-stripped ruin still costs something to finish; a free ruin would make the last few units of every berth a formality. The open question §3.11 records is whether the answer is ever genuinely "it depends" rather than always yes or always no; this is the constant that moves if it is not, and it should move together with the enclave's scrap-for-poles rate, never alone. |
| `warden_wake_paces` | 120 | DESIGNED | How far out of the ruin a warden surfaces. At a warden's own 20 paces per 100 ticks (Content constants below) that is 600 ticks — about twenty seconds between the ground moving and the first bite, against a stationary tower. Deliberately the same order of warning `spawn_paces_ahead` (540) buys an ordinary wave, but bought at a fifth of the distance, because the arithmetic is different in both directions: an ordinary wave closes at the creature's speed *plus* the tower's stride, while a berthed tower contributes nothing, and a warden is the slowest thing in the pack. 540 here would have been a minute and a half of nothing happening, which is a warning that has stopped being one. Twenty seconds is enough to notice the rig has woken something and decide whether to keep working or start walking — and that decision, taken under time pressure rather than at leisure, is the point of the number. |

| region `ruin_richness_min_pct` / `ruin_richness_max_pct` | 60–140%, both regions | DESIGNED | Rolled once per region and applied to what each of its ruins holds. This is the roll the player actually feels, because it changes whether stopping is worth it at all: one run's drowned city is picked over and grudging, the next one's is worth berthing at three times. Wide enough — better than a factor of two end to end — that a lean city and a generous one are different propositions rather than different rounding. It scales the *amount* rather than the count, so a poor region still has ruins in it, visibly near-empty ones, which reads as disappointment rather than as absence. §3.11's second open question notes the risk this carries: a mechanic worth it at 140% and never worth it at 60% is a mechanic that is off half the time, and if the balance pass finds that, the fix is a narrower range here or a cheaper warden, not a bigger payout. |

| region `threat_pct` | 100 / 150 | DESIGNED | A multiplier on the provocation-scaled threat budget, applied after the affordability gate and before `base_threat`'s floor. Region 2 is half again as dangerous for the same provocation — enough that arriving in the city with a defence that was adequate in the jungle is visibly not adequate any more, and not so much that it reads as a difficulty setting the player did not choose. One knob rather than a per-region creature table on purpose: M2 already recorded that most of the four-creature taxonomy is unexercised in play, and authoring selection rules for creatures a run has never met would be content for a system not yet consuming what it has (`SYSTEMS.md` §3.10). Per-region tables belong with M5's taxonomy pass. |
| region `fork_interval_paces` | 15,000, both regions | DESIGNED | Three forks across a midpoint-length region 1, about fourteen minutes apart at 1×. Punctuation, not a metronome: spacing is content rather than seed precisely so the player learns the rhythm of "another choice is coming" without having to watch for it, and a fork that could arrive at any moment would be an interruption rather than a beat. Region 2 keeps the same interval so the beat the player learned in the jungle still holds; a shorter region simply fits fewer of them, which together with `fork_edge_margin_paces` (Journey above) is how the length roll turns into a fork count. |
| branch `length_paces` | 6,000, every archetype | DESIGNED | Roughly five and a half minutes at 1× — long enough that a branch changes what the chain is doing (a mill's whole input mix, several charge cycles' worth of sun) rather than passing as a texture, and short enough that a bad pick is a bad few minutes rather than a lost region. Uniform across all six archetypes because the choice at a fork is meant to be *what* the next stretch is like, not *how long* you are committing for; a branch that was also longer would be two decisions wearing one card. Note it exceeds `fork_edge_margin_paces` (5,000), so a branch taken at the last fork in a region can run past the boundary — which is fine and intended: a branch is a palette override on one continuous axis, not a detour, so nothing downstream cares. |
| branch `threat_pct` | 80 / 100 / 130 | DESIGNED | Multiplied into the owning region's own `threat_pct`, so the quiet branch of the drowned city (150 × 80% = 120) is still louder than the loud branch of the jungle (100 × 130% = 130) is not — the two are nearly level, which is the right relationship: leaving the city's danger behind should take more than picking the calm option. The 80/130 spread is meaningfully asymmetric around 100 on purpose. The quieter branch is a real reprieve; the louder one is not punishing on its own, because a branch is chosen from a card two heaviest-terrain-kinds long and a player who picks "louder" for a better palette should be making a trade, not a mistake. Each region authors one of each plus a neutral, so a fork never offers the same pair twice running. |
| enclave `reinforce` (cost / `panel_hp` / `times`) | 18 scrap / 60 / 2 | DESIGNED | Briefly withdrawn on a measurement that turned out to be the harness's fault, not the feature's — see the note at the head of this section, which is worth reading before trusting any number in it. Shell work: the only permanent upgrade in the game, and the only thing scrap becomes other than a trade. Sixty hit points a round is 40% of a panel (`panel_hp` 150), so two rounds take every floor to 270 — nearly double, which is what makes region 2's half-again `threat_pct` land on a tower that can take it. | enclave `at_paces` | 8,000 into region 2 | DESIGNED | About seven minutes of 1× walking past the boundary — one or two of the city's ruins, so the normal case is arriving with something to trade, while the remaining four-fifths of the region is still ahead of you to be provisioned for. | enclave `offers` | 4 scrap → 3 poles ×20 · 10 bamboo → 4 poles ×10 · 4 poles → 6 darts ×12 | DESIGNED | Every rate is deliberately worse than the chain's own — the mill turns bamboo into poles one for one and the thornwright turns a pole into three darts — so the enclave is where a tower that lacks a room buys around the gap once, not a substitute for building the room.
