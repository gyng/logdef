# Understory — Balance

Every tuning constant in the game, with where it came from.

Values live in `assets/data/balance.ron`; this file grades them. A test
(`crates/core/src/tests/balance_doc.rs`) fails if a constant exists in one and not the
other, in either direction — so this table cannot quietly rot.

## Grades

| Grade | Means |
|---|---|
| `DESIGNED` | Chosen from reasoning about the intended feel. Plausible, unproven. |
| `PLAYTESTED` | Someone played with it, and with neighbouring values, and this one won. |

M5 does not ship until every row reads `PLAYTESTED`. Being wrong fast is the point of
having the table; being wrong *quietly* is what the grade prevents.

---

## World

The tower's gait and how far it can see.

| Constant | Value | Grade | Reasoning |
|---|---:|---|---|
| `stride_paces_per_100_ticks` | 60 | DESIGNED | 0.6 paces/tick, 18 a second. Fast enough that the horizon changes while you watch a chain run; slow enough that terrain feels like somewhere rather than something flickering past. Becomes a player throttle in M3. |
| `band_min_paces` | 300 | DESIGNED | ~16 s in a band at 1×. Below this, terrain changes faster than a haul round-trip, so route choice could never bite. |
| `band_max_paces` | 900 | DESIGNED | ~50 s at 1×, ~12 s at 4×. Long enough that a rich band is worth noticing and a barren one is worth waiting out. |
| `stream_ahead_paces` | 900 | DESIGNED | One full maximum band beyond the tower, so the horizon is never visibly generated into existence. |
| `stream_behind_paces` | 300 | DESIGNED | Enough to keep what has just passed on screen for parallax; anything older is dropped. |

## Tower

Shape, height, and the price of both.

| Constant | Value | Grade | Reasoning |
|---|---:|---|---|
| `starting_floors` | 4 | DESIGNED | Enough vertical distance that the opening haul is a climb, few enough that the tower reads at a glance on the first frame. |
| `max_floors` | 14 | DESIGNED | The top of the cozy 8–14 range from `v2-plan.md` §0. Above this the cross-section stops fitting on one screen, which is the constraint the whole art direction rests on. |
| `floor_slots` | 8 | DESIGNED | Four two-wide rooms, or three plus a shaft column. Tight enough that a shaft is a real sacrifice — which is the entire point of shafts costing floor width. |
| `floor_cost` | 6 poles | DESIGNED | About two minutes of the opening chain's output. Growing taller should be a decision you save up for, not a button you press. |
| `stairs_capacity` | 1 | DESIGNED | One body at a time. With two crew this is already a queue, which puts the contention thesis on screen in the first minute rather than in M1. |
| `starting_stock` | 10 poles | DESIGNED | One floor's worth plus a room, so the first build is possible before the mill has ever run, and the second is not. |

## Crew

How fast the tower's pulse beats.

| Constant | Value | Grade | Reasoning |
|---|---:|---|---|
| `crew_cap` | 6 | DESIGNED | The ceiling an enclave may recruit the tower up to. Twice the starting three, and short of the eight `v2-plan.md` §6.6 eventually wants, which M4's shift rota should have to earn rather than inherit. M3 can only move one place toward it — the drowned city's enclave offers exactly one recruit (see Content constants below) — so this is headroom for M4 and M5 rather than a limit a run will press against; it exists now because `Recruit` has to reject *something*, and "the tower is full" needs a number to be full against. Sized against `starting_crew`'s measurement rather than guessed: three crew moved throughput ninety percent over two, so a cap of six is two more doublings of that kind of change, which is as much as the stairs-and-shafts contention this game is built on should have to absorb before a shift rota exists to organise it. |
| `starting_crew` | 3 | PLAYTESTED | Was 2. Measured over 600 s (`cargo run --release -p understory-core --example throughput`): with two crew, adding an elevator changed crafted output by less than noise, so M1's sprint question could not be answered at all. At three, the same comparison shows **+90% crafts and 56% fewer ticks spent queueing**. Two crew make a queue; three make it matter. |
| `walk_ticks_per_slot` | 12 | DESIGNED | 0.4 s a slot, ~3 s to cross a floor. Horizontal distance is a real but minor cost, so layout matters without dominating. |
| `climb_ticks_per_floor` | 30 | DESIGNED | A full second a floor: two and a half times the cost of walking the same "distance". Vertical is expensive, which is why a dumbwaiter will be worth its slot in M1. |
| `load_ticks` | 15 | DESIGNED | Half a second at each end. Long enough to read as an action in the cross-section, short enough not to dominate a round trip. |
| `unload_ticks` | 15 | DESIGNED | Symmetric with loading; no reason yet for them to differ. |
| `carry_capacity` | 3 | DESIGNED | Sets haul throughput against production: two crew at three per trip roughly match the opening chain, so the tower runs but has no slack. That margin is the tension. |
| `stress_ticks` | 60 | DESIGNED | Two seconds blocked before a crew member tints red. Short enough to catch a real bottleneck, long enough that ordinary traffic doesn't cry wolf. |

## Clock

The day, and what reads it.

| Constant | Value | Grade | Reasoning |
|---|---:|---|---|
| `ticks_per_day` | 14400 | DESIGNED | 8 minutes at 1x (480 s x 30 ticks/s), ~2 minutes at 4x — about 20 day cycles across a 3-hour run, enough for the sun/route tension to repeat many times without a single day feeling like the whole session. |
| `sun_curve` | 8 anchors, 0-0-40-90-100-60-10-0 | DESIGNED | A smooth rise and fall rather than a staircase, so no daypart boundary reads as a lighting bug. Dark for the first tenth of the day, peaking at midday, dark again by the last fifteenth — integrating the curve over a full day (see Power below) gives an average exposure of ~46.5% on neutral terrain, the number the sail and cost figures below are sized against. |
| `night_light_threshold` | 20 | DESIGNED | Crossing this against the curve puts the "lights must be lit" span at ~330 permille of the day (~4,750 ticks, ~2.6 min) — long enough that unlit floors are a real cost, short enough that a competent tower shrugs off most nights. |

## Power

Charge income, storage, and draw. See `SYSTEMS.md` §1.2.

| Constant | Value | Grade | Reasoning |
|---|---:|---|---|
| `starting_charge` | 800 | DESIGNED | Tick 0 is predawn, so a fresh run starts in the dark. A starting tower's base draw (striding + lighting 4 floors, no thornwright yet) is ~0.28 charge/tick; 800 outlasts the ~2,200 ticks until the curve clears the light threshold, with room to spare, but is nowhere near enough to also run a thornwright unattended — the tower starts tight, not comfortable. |
| `stride_charge_per_100_ticks` | 20 | DESIGNED | Continuous striding costs 2,880 charge across a full day-cycle — real money next to a two-sail "good sun" day's ~20,000 charge income (see Content constants below), but not so much that walking is unaffordable. Visible and ongoing, which is what makes `SetStriding{false}` a genuine choice rather than a button nobody presses. |
| `light_charge_per_100_ticks_per_floor` | 2 | DESIGNED | Small per floor, but multiplies with height: an 8-floor tower lit through the ~4,750-tick night spends ~760 charge; a 4-floor starting tower spends ~380. Enough that a brown-out reads as "the tower outgrew what it can light," not as noise. |

## Transport

Dwell, dispatch, and the estimates crew use to pick a shaft. See `SYSTEMS.md` §1.3.

| Constant | Value | Grade | Reasoning |
|---|---:|---|---|
| `dwell_base_ticks` | 10 | DESIGNED | A third of a second of doors-and-landing overhead before anyone moves — enough to read as a stop in the cross-section, not enough to make every trip feel like a wait. |
| `dwell_per_unit_ticks` | 6 | DESIGNED | Cheaper than a full `load_ticks`/`unload_ticks` (15) — boarding a car is simpler than working a shelf — but it still adds up: a full 4-unit car adds 24 ticks to a stop, visibly slower than an empty one. |
| `dispatch_threshold` | 2 | DESIGNED | Matches `starting_crew`. With two crew, a car that leaves the instant the first caller arrives would never show contention at all, which is the exact thing M1's sprint question ("is elevator contention actually fun?") is testing. |
| `dispatch_max_wait_ticks` | 90 | DESIGNED | 3 seconds. A lone caller on a quiet floor still gets served eventually — batching is meant to create the queue a player can see, not to strand anyone waiting for a second caller who never comes. |
| `queue_penalty_ticks` | 60 | DESIGNED | A floor of 2 seconds per person ahead of you in a stairs estimate. Note the estimate multiplies this by the *queue length* rather than applying it once: a flat penalty had crew cheerfully joining a five-deep line because "occupied" cost the same whether one person or five were ahead. |
| `elevator_base_wait_ticks` | 45 | DESIGNED | 1.5 seconds, a rough midpoint between "the car is right here" and "the car is at the far end of its run." The estimate crew fall back on before the car's actual position and direction sharpen it. |

## Siege

What creatures attack, how far ahead they appear, how often waves come, the one knob
(provocation) that scales all of it, and what putting the tower back together costs. See
`SYSTEMS.md` §2.

| Constant | Value | Grade | Reasoning |
|---|---:|---|---|
| `enemy_fade_ticks` | 20 | DESIGNED | Two thirds of a second at 1x, still a sixth of one at 4x. Before this, a creature that died or lost its grip existed for exactly one further tick — one frame at 1x, likely zero frames at 4x — so a kill was a number changing, not something the player could watch happen. `Dying` (shot down) and `Leaving` (lost its grip — see the `cling_ticks` rows in Content constants, below) both hold for this long before they are removed, and fade identically: the point is giving the player a frame to see the outcome, not grading it. Grading is `repelled`'s job, and it only counts `Dying`. |
| `panel_hp` | 150 | DESIGNED | A floor's outer skin — the first thing a wave reaches and the cheapest thing to lose. Four skitters (the entire opening-wave budget, see Content constants below) breach an unanswered panel in well under a minute of unimpeded biting, which is the "an unprepared tower loses a panel" case this milestone is sized against. Repairing it from nothing costs 15 poles and 600 ticks of crew time — two and a half times a floor's own build cost (6 poles, see the Tower section above), a real but not crippling bill. |
| `room_hp` | 260 | DESIGNED | Not quite twice a panel's health. Rooms sit behind the skin, so reaching one should take real work once its floor's panel is already down, rather than the panel and the room behind it falling in the same few seconds. |
| `shaft_hp` | 600 | DESIGNED | Four times a panel's health. Severing a shaft is the signature emergency (`SYSTEMS.md` §2.3) and must never be one bite: a lone root-borer chewing uninterrupted (18 damage every 70 ticks) needs roughly 2,333 ticks — about 78 seconds — to take a shaft from full health to severed. That is real, sustained effort at one column, not a routine annoyance — and more than a lone borer actually gets to finish: its own `cling_ticks` (1,200, see Content constants below) runs out at roughly the halfway point, so on a tower that keeps walking, severing a shaft takes two overlapping borers, not one. |
| `heartseed_hp` | 4000 | DESIGNED | Well over six times a shaft column's health, and by far the toughest single thing in the tower. The Heartseed is the loss condition and is only ever targeted once nothing ordinary is left standing (`pick_target`'s fallback order) — nothing should reach it by accident, and nothing should finish it quickly once something does. |
| `spawn_paces_ahead` | 540 | DESIGNED | The tower strides 0.6 paces/tick (`stride_paces_per_100_ticks` above). 540 / 0.6 = 900 ticks = 30 s at 1x, comfortably inside a 20-40 s reaction window between the wave-arrival sound cue and contact. A creature's own approach speed adds to the tower's stride when closing the gap, so faster creatures (the night prowler) arrive sooner than this baseline and slower ones (the root-borer) later — the constant is sized against the tower's half of that closing speed, which is the one number every creature shares. |
| `wave_interval_ticks` | 4800 | PLAYTESTED | 160 seconds between wave checks — three a day-cycle (`ticks_per_day` 14400 / 4800), down from six. An opening-strength wave does about 192 damage, which costs about 19 poles to mend (`repair_poles_per_10_hp`, below); one mill makes about 20 poles every 2,400 ticks at its nominal craft rate, and fewer once haul latency is counted (see `provocation_per_100_harvested`, below, on how far short of nominal a mill actually runs). At the old 2,400-tick interval, a tower's entire pole income went straight back into its own skin every single wave, it could never bank enough to build anything else, and a damaged mill turned that into a spiral it could not climb out of — a recorded run against the old value sat at 0 poles in the bank against a 120-pole repair bill. The doubled interval is confirmed by the three-tower, five-day comparison in `cargo run -p understory-core --example siege_run` (see `provocation_per_100_harvested`, below, for the full shape of that run): even the undefended `greedy` tower, which goes pole-broke and stops repairing by day 4, is degraded rather than annihilated, and the `answered` tower holds full integrity through two days of rising provocation before it starts spending down. A five-day scripted run through one shopping list is one designed scenario, not a person probing the edges, so treat the grade as confirmed for the shape of play the harness exercises rather than exhaustively tuned. |
| `threat_per_100_provocation` | 40 | DESIGNED | Converts provocation into a wave's threat budget: `scaled = provocation * 40 / 100`. A wave check now spends `scaled` first against the cheapest eligible creature's threat — if it can't afford even that (skitter, threat 5, so below provocation 13) nothing spawns at all, which is the gate `base_threat` (below) used not to have. Once a wave clears that gate, the actual budget is `scaled.max(base_threat)`, not the two summed: at `provocation_max` (1000), `scaled` is 400 — forty times the `base_threat` floor of 10, and the richest wave the game throws. That escalation is what makes an aggressively-played, undefended run feel like it is heading somewhere bad, without the very first point of provocation buying a full wave the way an unconditional floor used to. |
| `base_threat` | 10 | PLAYTESTED | The floor under a wave's threat budget once one has already cleared the affordability gate in `threat_per_100_provocation` (above) — not, itself, a floor that guarantees a wave exists at all. With `threat_per_100_provocation` at 40 and the skitter (threat 5) the cheapest eligible creature, the earliest a wave can happen at all is provocation 13; from there, `base_threat` now guarantees the budget is never below 10, buying a pair of skitters rather than the four an unconditional floor of 20 used to guarantee from the very first point of provocation, regardless of how little the tower had done. That earlier version put a barely-harvesting tower under full siege before it had done anything worth answering for; halving the floor, on top of gating it behind an affordability check, means four skitters and beyond is now something a run earns by playing loudly, not the baseline every run opens with. The three-tower `siege_run` comparison (see `provocation_per_100_harvested`, below) is consistent with the smaller floor: none of the three towers — including `greedy`, which draws heavy provocation with no defence at all — loses a large share of its integrity to a single early wave; damage accrues gradually across days rather than arriving all at once. That is a shape the run's day-by-day standing figures support, not a direct measurement of wave composition, so the grade should be read with that limit in mind. |
| `provocation_max` | 1000 | DESIGNED | The ceiling every other provocation field is a fraction of. Round, so the canopy leaper's "about a third" (330) and the root-borer's "half" (500) gates land on clean numbers. |
| `provocation_per_100_harvested` | 300 | PLAYTESTED | Three points an item — measured against an actual tower, not derived from an arm's nominal rate. The original calibration (10, then 100) assumed a cutter arm's nominal output — one stalk per 90 ticks (`ticks_per_item`, see Content constants below), ~158 items a day — and set the gain to roughly cancel `provocation_decay_per_100_ticks` at that figure. Probed directly instead (a two-arm tower, seed 1001, no defences, no player intervention): throughput is bounded by shelf space and crew legs, not by the arm — the tower harvested only ~14 items per 1000 ticks (~202/day) while flowing, and stopped entirely at ~83 items once the storerooms filled. Against a decay tuned for the nominal rate, the dial could never turn: the probed two-arm tower peaked at provocation 14 and bled straight back to zero the moment it jammed. At 300, the same shape of tower nets roughly +318 a day and draws its first wave inside the first two minutes; a one-arm (default) tower nets about +15 a day, which is noise — a subsistence tower stays quiet, and the second arm is what provokes. Originally 10, which left decay outpacing a hard-harvesting tower tenfold so the knob never turned at all; raised to 100, still against the same unmeasured nominal rate, before the probe above replaced the assumption with a measurement. A fuller comparison confirms the shape holds across a real shopping list, not just a static probe: `cargo run -p understory-core --example siege_run` plays three towers from one seed over five in-game days — `subsistence` (one cutter arm, never expands), `greedy` (a second cutter arm and nothing else), and `answered` (the second arm plus a mill, a thornwright, and a dart battery) — each spending poles on a shopping list as it can afford to. `subsistence` peaks at provocation 11, just under the wave gate, and is left alone for the rest of the run, ending at full (1000‰) integrity with 220 poles banked and nothing left to spend them on. `greedy` — which doubles bamboo throughput without adding a second mill, so its pole income never rises to match — climbs to provocation 248 by day 3, degrades to 743‰ integrity, runs out of poles by day 2, and has stopped repairing entirely by day 4. `answered` pays for defence out of the same pole income and holds full (1000‰) integrity through two days of rising provocation, sees off 51 creatures, and is still mending on day 5, ending at 828‰. That three-way shape — quiet subsistence, punished but not destroyed greed, defence that visibly pays for itself — is what this constant, together with the decay rate below, is tuned to produce. It is one scripted five-day run rather than a person playing many, so the grade reflects confirmation for this shape of play, not exhaustive tuning against everything a real session might try. |
| `provocation_per_burn` | 18 | DESIGNED | The burner run through one full night stretch (~4,750 ticks below `night_light_threshold`, see the Clock section above, / 100 ticks per burn = ~47 burns) raises provocation by 47 x 18 = ~846 points that night — three times the 288-a-day the current decay sheds (see `provocation_decay_per_100_ticks`, below), so burning through the night still costs more than a day of quiet walking can pay back. Scaled up from 6 alongside `provocation_per_100_harvested`'s three-fold jump (100 to 300) to keep the burner the louder of the two sources by roughly the same margin as before, rather than an independent measurement — the probe behind the harvested-rate change (see that row) never toggled the burner. |
| `provocation_per_100_salvaged` | 300 | DESIGNED | Three points a unit — deliberately the *same* rate as `provocation_per_100_harvested` (above) rather than a louder one. A ruin already charges for itself: rousing it fields wardens worth 120 threat per 100 units of salvage (`warden_threat_per_100_salvage`, Journey below), which at 30 threat apiece is one warden per 25 units, and a warden costs 20 darts and about 16 poles of mending to see off. That is the price of the ruin. Stacking a second, much louder price in provocation on top would push the total cost of one berth past what any single ruin can pay back, and salvage becomes a thing a player does once and never again — which would waste the whole of §3.4. Setting it equal to cutting instead means a 40-unit ruin (120 points, a little under half a day's decay at `provocation_decay_per_100_ticks` 2) reads as "you were noisy for a few minutes", the same order of noise as running a second cutter arm for the same stretch. The one thing to watch in the balance pass is that berthing *also* stops the cutter arms (per-pace intake, `SYSTEMS.md` §3.6), so a tower that stops to salvage is trading one provocation source for another rather than adding to it — the net may well come out quieter than walking on, and if it does, this rate is the lever to raise, not the wardens. |
| `provocation_decay_per_100_ticks` | 2 | PLAYTESTED | A full day of decay alone is 14,400/100 x 2 = 288 points — a little under a third of the ceiling. Doubled alongside the harvested-rate recalibration above so a subsistence tower (one arm, ~101 items/day, ~+15 net) still reads as quiet rather than slowly climbing, while a flowing two-arm tower (~+318 net) still reaches the ceiling in a handful of days rather than one. Visible directly in the same probe cited under `provocation_per_100_harvested`: once the storerooms fill around tick 9000 and harvesting stops, decay alone pulls provocation from 66 back down to 6 by tick 12000 — 60 points over 3000 ticks, exactly 2 per 100, with nothing else acting on it. A tower that stops provoking and just walks sheds 288 points a day instead of climbing, which is the visible bleed-off quiet play is supposed to show. The three-tower `siege_run` comparison cited under `provocation_per_100_harvested` (above) shows the same dial working across a full run rather than one probe: `greedy`'s provocation climbs 131 → 227 → 248 across its first three days while it is still harvesting hard, then collapses to 0 by day 4 once it is pole-broke, room-damaged, and effectively stopped producing — decay alone pulling it back down with nothing offsetting it. `subsistence`, which never provokes much in the first place, is down to 0 by day 2. That is the "a tower that stops provoking is back to quiet" case this constant is sized for, now shown across a full run — still one scripted script rather than a played session, so the grade should be read as confirmed for that shape, not tuned against every way a person might play. |
| `repair_ticks_per_hp` | 4 | DESIGNED | About a seventh of a second a hit point. Fully repairing a severed shaft (600 hp) costs 2,400 ticks — 80 seconds of one crew member's dedicated time, real competition against hauling rather than a button press. |
| `repair_poles_per_10_hp` | 1 | PLAYTESTED | A fully severed shaft (600 hp) costs 60 poles to rebuild; a breached panel (150 hp) costs 15. Repair stays dearer per unit than building fresh — a floor costs 6 poles all in (see the Tower section above) — because it is fiddly work on something already standing, but now only about twice as dear rather than many times over. Originally 2: a panel alone cost 30 poles, five times a whole floor, and a tower repairing at the ordinary rate spent every pole one mill could make on patching its own skin and could never save enough to build anything else — measured directly, a recorded run sat at 3-4 poles in the bank indefinitely, unable to afford an 18-pole elevator after 20,000 ticks. Halving it puts an undefended, regularly-harassed tower at roughly break-even: it can survive indefinitely by patching, but it will not grow until it answers the jungle some other way. Repair still has to compete with hauling and with building for the same stock, not win by default, which is the whole point of triage as a decision (`SYSTEMS.md` §2.5). The three-tower `siege_run` comparison cited under `provocation_per_100_harvested` (above) confirms the rate under real, continuous damage rather than a single probe: `greedy` — undefended, and taking far heavier damage than `answered` — has its crew mend 668, 1,248, and 1,050 hit points across its first three days for 72, 132, and 106 poles respectively, real, working repair at this rate, but the damage keeps arriving faster than one mill's pole income can pay for, the tower runs down to 1 pole banked by day 3, and repair simply stops — mended and spent both go to 0 from day 4 on, with an outstanding bill that only climbs, ending at 186 poles. `answered`, defended and so taking far less damage per day, repairs at a comparable rate but never runs dry: it is still mending on day 5 and ends with an outstanding bill of 138 poles rather than an empty bank and a stalled crew. That is the intended shape — this rate is affordable for a tower that has also paid to keep the damage down, and a real, visible cost for one that hasn't — confirmed by one scripted run rather than by a person probing the rate against neighbouring values, which is what the grade here should be taken to mean. |
| `repair_hp_per_shift` | 20 | DESIGNED | One shift is 20 hp: 80 ticks of a crew member's time and 2 poles. Chunky on purpose — a crew member who commits to a repair is off the haul rota for a noticeable stretch, and that absence is what makes triage a decision rather than a formality. Repairing a hit point at a time would let them dip in and out for free. This is also the floor below which crew will not start a repair at all: a shift costs its poles whether it mends twenty hit points or one, so `pick_repair` skips anything missing less than a full shift's worth — a tower that started jobs on every scratch was spending its stock on marks too small to matter, often enough that it could never bank poles for anything else. Below a shift's worth of damage, the mark just stays on the tower, which is the cross-section doing its job as the health readout rather than a leak. |
| `wrecked_permille` | 250 | DESIGNED | A quarter health. A room keeps limping along above this line — damaged and slower, but still contributing — and only goes fully silent once it crosses it, so the player sees a visible decline before the room stops rather than a surprise cliff. |

## Journey

The handful of constants that belong to the run rather than to any one room or creature:
where a fork is allowed to fall, how near an enclave counts as berthed, and what a ruin
charges for what it holds. See `SYSTEMS.md` §3.3–§3.5.

| Constant | Value | Grade | Reasoning |
|---|---:|---|---|
| `fork_edge_margin_paces` | 5000 | DESIGNED | A third of a fork interval (15,000, see Content constants below). A fork inside this margin of either end of its region, or of the region's enclave, is skipped — a decision that lands on top of a boundary or a berth competes with it for the same stretch of horizon, and the player ends up answering two things at once when the whole argument for a *fixed* fork interval was that the rhythm should be learnable. Sized by the two cases it actually has to arbitrate. Region 2's enclave stands 8,000 paces in and its first fork at 15,000: 7,000 apart, so the margin has to be under that or the region's only fork disappears. And region 2's second fork at 30,000 against a length roll of 34,000–46,000 is 4,000 from the far edge at the short end and 16,000 at the long end, so a margin of 5,000 is exactly what makes the roll decide whether that fork exists — which is the mechanism §3.2 leans on to buy variety without making fork spacing itself unpredictable. Both jobs at once, from one number, is why it is 5,000 and not 3,000 or 8,000. |
| `enclave_berth_paces` | 80 | DESIGNED | About a five-second window at 1× and under two at 4× — the tower covers 80 paces in 133 ticks at `stride_paces_per_100_ticks` 60. A shade wider than the salvage rig's own 60-pace reach (Content constants below), because a rig is a room the player deliberately placed and an enclave is a thing the world put in front of them: the berth you have to *notice* deserves a slightly larger target than the one you built for. Not wide enough to stop by accident, which matters, since the enclave is behind you for the rest of the run once passed. If it reads as fiddly in play, the fix is a larger number here — not an approach-and-dock mechanic, which would make berthing a mode and cost §3.5 its "the transaction is a board, the berth is diegetic" split. |
| `warden_threat_per_100_salvage` | 120 | DESIGNED | The one number that prices a ruin, and it prices both sides at once. At a warden's threat of 30 (Content constants below), 120 per 100 units means one warden per 25 units of salvage: the 25-unit floor of a ruin's authored range rouses exactly one, a 50-unit ruin rouses two, and the top of the range at full richness rouses a handful. The payout and the price are therefore the *same* number, which is what makes "is this ruin worth stopping for" a question about the tower — its darts, its poles, its remaining panel health — rather than a question about the ruin, which would have a fixed answer. Floored at a single warden so a nearly-stripped ruin still costs something to finish; a free ruin would make the last few units of every berth a formality. The open question §3.11 records is whether the answer is ever genuinely "it depends" rather than always yes or always no; this is the constant that moves if it is not, and it should move together with the enclave's scrap-for-poles rate, never alone. |
| `warden_wake_paces` | 120 | DESIGNED | How far out of the ruin a warden surfaces. At a warden's own 20 paces per 100 ticks (Content constants below) that is 600 ticks — about twenty seconds between the ground moving and the first bite, against a stationary tower. Deliberately the same order of warning `spawn_paces_ahead` (540) buys an ordinary wave, but bought at a fifth of the distance, because the arithmetic is different in both directions: an ordinary wave closes at the creature's speed *plus* the tower's stride, while a berthed tower contributes nothing, and a warden is the slowest thing in the pack. 540 here would have been a minute and a half of nothing happening, which is a warning that has stopped being one. Twenty seconds is enough to notice the rig has woken something and decide whether to keep working or start walking — and that decision, taken under time pressure rather than at leisure, is the point of the number. |

## Content constants

Room, item, terrain, creature, and region numbers live next to their definitions under
`assets/data/` and are graded here as a group, since they are tuned as a set rather than
individually.

|---|---:|---|---|
| cutter arm `paces_per_item` | 54 | DESIGNED | Per **pace walked**, not per tick — the riskiest change in M3 (`SYSTEMS.md` §3.6) and the one that makes stopping cost something. 54 is exactly the old `ticks_per_item` of 90 converted at the tower's 0.6 paces/tick (`stride_paces_per_100_ticks` 60), so a tower that never stops harvests at precisely the M2 rate: one stalk per 3 s of walking at ordinary yield, 2.1 s under canopy, 6 s across a ruin-field. | thornwright `craft_ticks` | 150 | DESIGNED | 5 s a dart — nominally slower than the mill's 4 s (mill supplies 1 pole/120 ticks against a thornwright's demand of 1/150), which was originally read as meaning a single thornwright could never out-consume a single mill's output. The fix was not a slower thornwright but a smaller yield per pole (see `thornwright recipe`, below): craft speed stays as designed here, for the reason stated; it was the 1-pole-to-1-dart ratio built on top of it that didn't hold, and that ratio is what has since changed, not this timing. |

| thornwright recipe (poles → darts) / output `buffer_max` | 1 → 3; 9 | PLAYTESTED | Was 1 → 1, with an output buffer of 6. At one dart a pole, a thornwright converts a mill's entire realised output straight into darts: measured directly, about 96 poles became 96 darts in a day, while a dart battery answering a normal run's waves spent barely forty of them (`assets/data/rooms/thornwright.ron`) — the surplus had nowhere to go but the storeroom, and a tower running both a thornwright and taking damage could not also afford to mend itself. Splitting a pole three ways leaves most of a mill's output free for repair while keeping shooting the cheap choice: a skitter costs 2 darts to put down and does 48 hit points of damage over a full, unanswered cling — `repair_poles_per_10_hp` (above) prices that at roughly 5 poles — so killing stays cheaper than the bite. The `answered` tower in the three-tower `siege_run` comparison (see `provocation_per_100_harvested`, above) runs a thornwright continuously at this ratio for all five days and is still repairing and repelling on day 5 rather than sitting on an unspent dart glut or going pole-broke the way the undefended `greedy` tower does — consistent with, though not an independent replication of, the direct poles-to-darts measurement above. |
